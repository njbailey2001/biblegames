The Cyrus Cylinder

This reading opens on Wednesday, November 4, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A Persian king's clay announcement helps explain how the Jewish exiles were able to go home.

Read alongside: Ezra 1:1–4; Isaiah 44:28–45:1; 2 Chronicles 36:22–23

Object file
What
Barrel-shaped clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform
Dated
Shortly after 539 BC
Found
Babylon, Iraq, 1879, by Hormuzd Rassam
Now at
British Museum, London

In 539 BC, Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon, the empire that had destroyed Jerusalem and its temple in 586 BC. For the Jewish people living in exile there, this was a turning point. Within a short time, many were allowed to return home and rebuild. A small clay object found in the ruins of Babylon helps us understand how that happened.

The discovery

In 1879, Assyrian-born archaeologist Hormuzd Rassam, working for the British Museum, found a clay cylinder about 23 centimeters (9 inches) long at Babylon. It is covered in Akkadian cuneiform, the writing system of Mesopotamia. Cylinders like this were often buried in the foundations of buildings as a record for future kings and the gods.

What it says

The text first criticizes Nabonidus, the last Babylonian king, for neglecting the worship of Marduk, the chief god of Babylon. It then says Marduk chose Cyrus to rule and let him enter Babylon without a battle. Cyrus speaks in the first person, listing his royal titles and describing how he brought peace to the city.

The most important part for Bible readers comes near the end. Cyrus says he returned the images of gods to their sanctuaries in various cities and gathered their former inhabitants and returned them to their homes. In other words, Cyrus had a general policy of letting displaced peoples go back to their homelands and restore their local temples.

Connecting to Ezra

Ezra 1:1–4 records a decree of Cyrus allowing the Jews to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the house of the Lord, and ordering that the temple vessels Nebuchadnezzar took be given back. The Cyrus Cylinder does not mention the Jews, Judah or Jerusalem. It focuses on cities in Mesopotamia. But it shows that the kind of decree Ezra describes fits exactly how Cyrus governed.

There is a striking difference in language. On the cylinder, Cyrus credits Marduk. In Ezra, Cyrus says the Lord, the God of heaven, gave him the kingdoms of the earth. Ancient rulers often spoke respectfully of each local people's god as a way to win their loyalty, so this is not surprising.

Isaiah's prophecy

For believers, the most remarkable connection is Isaiah 44:28 and 45:1, where God names Cyrus as his shepherd and his anointed, who will say of Jerusalem, "She shall be built," and of the temple, "Your foundation shall be laid." Isaiah describes God using a foreign king who did not know him (Isaiah 45:4) to carry out his purposes for his people.

The exiles who returned faced a hard road, as the books of Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai and Zechariah show. But the door home was opened by a Persian king whose own record still survives.
