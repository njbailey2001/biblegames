The Book of Jonah

This reading opens on Friday, November 6, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A runaway prophet, a great fish, and a city of enemies who repent. The surprise ending is the point.

Read alongside: Jonah 1–4; 2 Kings 14:25; Matthew 12:39–41

Book at a glance
Setting
8th century BC, in the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (c. 793–753 BC)
Length
4 chapters, 48 verses
Places
Joppa, the sea, Nineveh (capital region of Assyria, near modern Mosul, Iraq)
Key idea
God's mercy reaches even Israel's enemies

Jonah son of Amittai was a real prophet in the northern kingdom of Israel. 2 Kings 14:25 says he prophesied that King Jeroboam II would restore Israel's borders, which happened. His own book, though, is mostly about a mission he did not want.

Chapter 1: Running from God

God told Jonah to go to Nineveh, a great city of Assyria, and preach against its wickedness. Assyria was Israel's dangerous enemy, known for brutal warfare. Jonah went the opposite direction. He went down to the port of Joppa and bought passage on a ship to Tarshish, a distant place far to the west, probably in Spain.

The Lord sent a violent storm. The pagan sailors prayed to their gods and threw cargo overboard while Jonah slept below deck. They cast lots to find out who was to blame, and the lot fell on Jonah. He admitted he worshiped the Lord, the God of heaven who made the sea and the dry land, and told them to throw him into the sea. They tried to row to shore first, then reluctantly threw him over. The sea grew calm, and the sailors feared the Lord and offered sacrifices to him.

Chapter 2: Inside the fish

God appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah, and he was inside it three days and three nights. From there Jonah prayed a psalm of thanksgiving, describing sinking into the deep and being rescued. He ends with the confession that salvation belongs to the Lord. Then the fish spat Jonah out on dry land.

Chapter 3: Nineveh repents

God gave his command a second time, and this time Jonah obeyed. Nineveh was so large it took three days to cross. Jonah's message was short: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown." The people believed God. Everyone from the greatest to the least put on sackcloth and fasted. The king himself sat in ashes and ordered everyone, even the animals, to fast and turn from violence. When God saw that they turned from their evil ways, he relented and did not bring the disaster.

Chapter 4: An angry prophet

Instead of rejoicing, Jonah was furious. He finally explained why he had run: he knew God is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love (echoing Exodus 34:6), and he did not want God to spare Nineveh. Jonah sat outside the city to see what would happen. God made a leafy plant grow to shade him, then sent a worm to kill it, and a hot east wind. Jonah was angry enough to want to die over the plant.

God's final words end the book with a question. Jonah cared about a plant he did not plant or grow. Should God not care about Nineveh, with more than 120,000 people who do not know their right hand from their left, and also many animals? The book never tells us how Jonah answered. Each reader is left to answer for themselves.

Jonah and Jesus

Jesus referred to Jonah directly. In Matthew 12:39–41, he said the only sign given to his generation would be the sign of Jonah: as Jonah was three days and three nights in the fish, the Son of Man would be in the heart of the earth. He added that the people of Nineveh repented at Jonah's preaching, and someone greater than Jonah was now here.
