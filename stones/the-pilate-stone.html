The Pilate Stone

This reading opens on Saturday, November 7, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A reused block from a Roman theater carries the name of the governor who sentenced Jesus.

Read alongside: Matthew 27:11–26; Luke 3:1; John 18:28–19:16

Object file
What
Limestone block with a Latin dedication, about 82 x 65 cm
Dated
AD 26–36, Pilate's term in Judea
Found
Caesarea Maritima, Israel, 1961, by an Italian team led by Antonio Frova
Now at
Israel Museum, Jerusalem (a replica stands at Caesarea)

Pontius Pilate is one of the best-known names in history. Christians around the world say it in the Apostles' Creed: Jesus "suffered under Pontius Pilate." Roman and Jewish writers mention him too, including the historian Tacitus, the Jewish historian Josephus, and the philosopher Philo of Alexandria. Yet until 1961, no inscription from Pilate's own time had been found bearing his name.

Found in a theater

Caesarea Maritima was a grand port city on the Mediterranean coast built by Herod the Great. After Judea came under direct Roman rule in AD 6, Caesarea became the headquarters of the Roman governors. Acts 23 and 25 describe Paul being held there before governors Felix and Festus.

In 1961, Italian archaeologists led by Antonio Frova were excavating the city's Roman theater. They found a limestone block that had been reused, centuries later, as a step during a rebuilding of the theater. One side had been trimmed, and part of the text was lost. But the remaining Latin letters were clear enough.

What it says

The surviving words are usually read as: "...Tiberieum ... [Pon]tius Pilatus ... [Praef]ectus Iuda[ea]e." In English: Pontius Pilate, Prefect of Judea, dedicated a Tiberieum. A Tiberieum was probably a building or structure honoring the emperor Tiberius, who reigned AD 14–37. Luke 3:1 dates the start of John the Baptist's ministry to the fifteenth year of Tiberius, "Pontius Pilate being governor of Judea." The stone fits that setting exactly.

Prefect, not procurator

The inscription also settled a small historical question. Tacitus, writing decades later, called Pilate a "procurator." The stone shows that his official title was "prefect," a military title used for governors of Judea in that period. The title of procurator became standard for later governors. The Gospels use a general Greek word for governor, which fits either title.

Who Pilate was

Pilate governed Judea for about ten years, from AD 26 to 36. Josephus and Philo describe him as stubborn and harsh. He once brought military standards with the emperor's image into Jerusalem, angering the Jewish people, and he used temple funds to build an aqueduct. Luke 13:1 mentions Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed with their sacrifices. He was eventually recalled to Rome after his troops attacked a group of Samaritans.

The Gospels show Pilate at his most important moment: questioning Jesus, finding no guilt in him, yet giving in to pressure and handing him over to be crucified. The stone from Caesarea reminds us that this trial happened in real history, under a real Roman official.
