The Merneptah Stele: Israel's First Mention

This reading opens on Thursday, November 5, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A pharaoh's victory poem contains the oldest known reference to Israel outside the Bible.

Read alongside: Judges 1–2; Exodus 1:8–11

Object file
What
Granite stele about 3 m (10 ft) tall, with an Egyptian hieroglyphic inscription
Dated
c. 1208 BC (5th year of Pharaoh Merneptah)
Found
Thebes (near modern Luxor), Egypt, 1896, by Flinders Petrie
Now at
Egyptian Museum, Cairo

In 1896, British archaeologist Flinders Petrie was excavating the remains of a funerary temple of Pharaoh Merneptah at Thebes in Egypt. His team uncovered a large black granite slab covered in hieroglyphs. When a colleague translated it, one word stood out. Petrie reportedly said this stele would be better known in the world than anything else he had found. He was right.

A pharaoh's boast

Merneptah was the son and successor of Ramesses II, one of Egypt's most famous pharaohs. The stele celebrates Merneptah's victory over the Libyans and their allies. Most of the long text is about that war. But near the end comes a short poem about lands to the northeast, in Canaan. Translated, it says something like this: Canaan has been plundered; Ashkelon has been carried off; Gezer has been captured; Yanoam is made as though it never existed; Israel is laid waste, his seed is no more.

Why the word "Israel" matters

Egyptian writing used signs called determinatives to show what kind of thing a word was. Ashkelon, Gezer and Yanoam are marked with the sign for a city-state or foreign land. Israel is marked with the sign for a people. So around 1208 BC, Egypt knew of a group called Israel living in Canaan, important enough to be listed alongside major city-states, but not yet organized as a kingdom with a fixed territory.

That fits well with the picture in the Book of Judges, when the tribes of Israel lived in the hill country of Canaan as a loose group without a king. The stele is about 3,200 years old and is the earliest known mention of Israel outside the Bible.

The claim of total destruction

Merneptah claimed Israel's "seed" was wiped out. Pharaohs commonly made extreme boasts like this. History shows the claim was wrong. Israel not only survived; it became a kingdom, and the people of Israel are still here today. For believers, the stele is a reminder of God's promise to preserve the descendants of Abraham.

What it does and doesn't tell us

The stele does not mention the Exodus, Moses, or Israel ever being in Egypt. What it does show is that by about 1208 BC Israel was already established in Canaan. That means any Exodus and settlement had to happen before then, which is one reason the stele is central to discussions about when the Exodus happened.
