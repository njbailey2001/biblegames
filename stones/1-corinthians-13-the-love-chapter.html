1 Corinthians 13: The Love Chapter

This reading opens on Monday, November 23, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

Paul's portrait of love, written to a gifted but divided church.

Read alongside: 1 Corinthians 12–14; John 13:34–35; 1 John 4:7–8

Chapter at a glance
Written by
Paul, from Ephesus (1 Corinthians 16:8)
Written to
The church at Corinth
Length
13 verses
Key word
Love (Greek agapē)

First Corinthians 13 is one of the most quoted chapters in the Bible. It is often read at weddings, but Paul first wrote it to a church with problems. The believers in Corinth were divided into groups, proud of their spiritual gifts, and competing with one another. In chapters 12 and 14, Paul writes about spiritual gifts and how to use them in worship. Chapter 13 sits between them and shows what should guide every gift: love.

Without love (verses 1–3)

Paul begins by imagining the greatest gifts and sacrifices. He could speak with the tongues of men and of angels, understand all mysteries, have faith to move mountains, give away everything he owns, and even give his body to be burned. But without love, he says, he would be only a noisy gong or a clanging cymbal. He would be nothing and gain nothing.

What love does (verses 4–7)

Next, Paul describes love by what it does and does not do. Love is patient and kind. It does not envy or boast, and it is not proud. It is not rude or self-seeking. It is not easily angered and keeps no record of wrongs. It does not rejoice in wrongdoing but rejoices in the truth. It "beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things" (1 Corinthians 13:7).

Each line answers a problem in Corinth. Believers there were proud, quarreling, and taking one another to court. Paul's picture of love shows a different way to live together.

Love never fails (verses 8–12)

Gifts such as prophecy, tongues and knowledge are for this present time, Paul says, and they will pass away. Love will not. Now we know only in part. "For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known" (verse 12). Ancient mirrors were made of polished metal and gave a dim reflection.

The greatest (verse 13)

The chapter closes: "And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity." The King James Version uses the word charity, from the Latin caritas. Most modern translations say love.

The word for love

Paul uses the Greek word agapē throughout the chapter. In the New Testament it often describes self-giving love, the kind of love God shows. Jesus told his disciples, "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you" (John 13:34). And John writes, "God is love" (1 John 4:8).

Why it matters

First Corinthians 13 reminds readers that talent, knowledge and even great sacrifice are not enough on their own. Paul points to love as the way that lasts.
