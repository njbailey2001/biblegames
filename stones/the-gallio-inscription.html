The Gallio Inscription

This reading opens on Sunday, November 22, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A letter from Emperor Claudius carved at Delphi names Gallio, the Roman governor Paul stood before in Corinth.

Read alongside: Acts 18:1–17

Object file
What
Fragments of a letter from Emperor Claudius carved in stone, in Greek
Dated
AD 52
Found
Delphi, Greece; first published in 1905
Now at
The Delphi Archaeological Museum

Delphi, in central Greece, was famous in the ancient world for its temple of Apollo and its oracle. Cities and rulers often set up inscriptions there. When French archaeologists excavated Delphi, they found fragments of a stone carved with a letter from the Roman emperor Claudius to the people of Delphi.

Gallio, proconsul of Achaia

The letter is broken into several pieces, and parts are missing. But one line is clear. In it, Claudius refers to "Junius Gallio, my friend and proconsul." A proconsul was the Roman governor of a province. Gallio governed Achaia, the province of southern Greece, whose capital was Corinth.

Gallio is known from other ancient writers too. He was the older brother of the famous Roman philosopher Seneca, who tutored the young Nero.

Dating the letter

Claudius's letter includes his royal titles, including a number that counts how many times he had been acclaimed as emperor after military victories. Other Roman inscriptions show when he received each acclamation. This places the letter in AD 52. Roman proconsuls of senatorial provinces normally served one-year terms, which places Gallio's time in Corinth around AD 51 to 52.

Gallio in the book of Acts

Acts 18 tells about Paul's stay in Corinth. He worked as a tentmaker with Aquila and Priscilla, taught in the synagogue, and stayed a year and a half. Then, "when Gallio was the deputy of Achaia," some of Paul's opponents brought him before the governor's judgment seat, accusing him of persuading people to worship God contrary to the law (Acts 18:12–13).

Before Paul could even speak, Gallio dismissed the case. He said that if it were a matter of wrongdoing or crime he would hear it, but since it was about words, names and their own law, they should settle it themselves. "I will be no judge of such matters," he said, and drove them from the judgment seat (Acts 18:14–16). The judgment seat, called the bema, still stands in the forum of ancient Corinth.

Why it matters

The Gallio inscription is one of the most helpful finds for dating the life of Paul. Because it fixes when Gallio was governor, it places Paul's time in Corinth around AD 50 to 52. From there, many other events in Acts and in Paul's letters can be placed in order. Luke used the correct title for Gallio, proconsul, and named the right man in the right place.
