Psalm 100: Enter His Gates with Thanksgiving

This reading opens on Wednesday, November 25, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

A five-verse call to thank God, sung in worship for three thousand years.

Read alongside: Psalm 100; Psalm 95:1–7; 1 Chronicles 16:34

Chapter at a glance
Heading
"A Psalm of praise" (in Hebrew, a psalm for thanksgiving)
Length
5 verses
Book
Psalms, Book 4 (Psalms 90–106)
Famous hymn
"All People That on Earth Do Dwell" (1561)

Psalm 100 is one of the shortest psalms, only five verses long. Its Hebrew heading calls it a psalm for todah, a word that means both "thanksgiving" and a thank offering brought to the temple. It may have been sung as worshipers came to the temple to give thanks to God.

A call to all the earth (verses 1–2)

The psalm opens with a call not just to Israel but to the whole world: "Make a joyful noise unto the Lord, all ye lands." It continues, "Serve the Lord with gladness: come before his presence with singing." Worship here is joyful and loud, not quiet or gloomy.

Know who he is (verse 3)

The center of the psalm explains the reason for praise: "Know ye that the Lord he is God: it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture." God is the Creator, and he cares for his people like a shepherd cares for his flock. This verse echoes Psalm 95:7, "For he is our God; and we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand."

Come through the gates (verse 4)

Verse 4 pictures worshipers arriving at the temple in Jerusalem: "Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise: be thankful unto him, and bless his name." The temple had outer gates and courts where the people gathered. The psalm invites them to come in giving thanks.

Why give thanks (verse 5)

The psalm ends with the reason for thanksgiving: "For the Lord is good; his mercy is everlasting; and his truth endureth to all generations." The word translated mercy is the Hebrew hesed, God's steadfast, loyal love. Similar words appear in many places in the Old Testament, such as 1 Chronicles 16:34 and Psalm 136, where the refrain "for his mercy endureth for ever" is repeated in every verse.

The Old Hundredth

In 1561, William Kethe wrote a rhyming English version of Psalm 100 that begins, "All people that on earth do dwell, sing to the Lord with cheerful voice." It was set to a tune from the Genevan Psalter. The tune became known as the "Old Hundredth" because of this psalm. The same tune is used for the Doxology, "Praise God, from whom all blessings flow," which is still sung in many churches today.

A psalm for Thanksgiving week

Psalm 100 is often read at Thanksgiving services. Its message is simple: God made us, God cares for us, and his love lasts forever, so come into his presence with thanks.
