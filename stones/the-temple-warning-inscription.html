The Temple Warning Inscription

This reading opens on Wednesday, November 11, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A stone sign from Herod's Temple threatened death to any Gentile who went too far. It helps explain a riot in Acts and a key verse in Ephesians.

Read alongside: Acts 21:26–36; Ephesians 2:11–22

Object file
What
Limestone block with a Greek warning inscription (one complete, one partial)
Dated
Herod's Temple, late 1st century BC – AD 70
Found
Jerusalem: complete stone 1871 (Charles Clermont-Ganneau); partial stone 1935
Now at
Istanbul Archaeology Museums (complete); Israel Museum, Jerusalem (partial)

Herod the Great began a massive rebuilding of the Jerusalem temple around 20 or 19 BC. The project continued long after his death; John 2:20 mentions that it had already taken forty-six years. The result was one of the most impressive buildings in the Roman world, set on an enormous platform still partly visible today, including the Western Wall.

Courts and boundaries

The temple complex was arranged in zones of increasing holiness. The large outer area, often called the Court of the Gentiles, was open to everyone, including non-Jews. Further in were courts for Jewish women, Jewish men and priests, and finally the sanctuary itself. Between the outer court and the inner courts stood a low stone barrier. Jewish writings call it the soreg.

The Jewish historian Josephus, who saw the temple himself, wrote that stone markers were set at intervals along this barrier, warning in Greek and Latin that no foreigner could go farther.

The stone is found

In 1871, French scholar Charles Clermont-Ganneau found one of those warning stones built into a later structure near the Temple Mount. It is a complete block with seven lines of Greek. In paraphrase, it says that no foreigner may enter within the barrier and enclosure around the temple, and that anyone caught doing so will be responsible for his own death, which will follow. The stone was taken to Constantinople (Istanbul), where it remains.

In 1935, a broken piece of a second warning stone was found outside the Lions' Gate of the Old City. Traces of red paint remain in its letters, showing how the words were made to stand out. It is displayed at the Israel Museum.

A riot in Acts 21

This inscription explains a dangerous moment in Paul's life. In Acts 21, Paul was in the temple completing a purification vow. Some Jews from Asia saw him and shouted that he had brought Greeks into the temple and defiled it. They had earlier seen him in the city with Trophimus, a Gentile from Ephesus, and assumed Paul had taken him inside the barrier. The whole city was stirred up, and the crowd dragged Paul out and tried to kill him. Roman soldiers had to rescue him (Acts 21:27–32). Given what the warning stones said, the crowd believed the penalty was death.

The dividing wall torn down

A few years later, Paul wrote to the church at Ephesus, the hometown of Trophimus. In Ephesians 2:14, he says Christ himself is our peace, who has made Jews and Gentiles one and has broken down "the dividing wall of hostility." In the temple, a stone wall and a warning of death kept Gentiles out. In Christ, Paul says, Gentiles who were once far off have been brought near, and both have access to the Father by one Spirit (Ephesians 2:13, 18).
