The Dead Sea Scrolls

How a shepherd's discovery in the Judean desert gave us Bible manuscripts a thousand years older than any known before.

Read alongside: Isaiah 40:8; Isaiah 53

Object file
What
Roughly 900+ manuscripts and fragments in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek
Dated
c. 250 BC – AD 68
Found
Eleven caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea, 1947–1956
Now at
Israel Museum, Jerusalem (Shrine of the Book) and the Israel Antiquities Authority

Late in 1946 or early in 1947, a young Bedouin shepherd named Muhammad edh-Dhib was searching for a lost animal in the cliffs above the northwest shore of the Dead Sea. He tossed a stone into a cave opening and heard pottery break. Inside were clay jars holding old leather scrolls wrapped in linen. Those first seven scrolls began one of the most important discoveries in the history of the Bible.

What was found

Between 1947 and 1956, Bedouin searchers and archaeologists explored eleven caves near a ruined settlement called Khirbet Qumran. Together the caves held the remains of more than 900 manuscripts. Most survive only as fragments, some no bigger than a postage stamp. They were written mostly in Hebrew, with some in Aramaic and Greek, and they date from about the third century BC to the first century AD.

About a quarter of the manuscripts are copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, the Christian Old Testament. Fragments of every book have been identified except Esther. Psalms, Deuteronomy and Isaiah are the most common, which matches how often Jesus and the New Testament writers quote those same books. The rest of the collection includes commentaries on Scripture, hymns, community rules, and other Jewish writings from the period.

The Great Isaiah Scroll

The most famous scroll is the Great Isaiah Scroll from Cave 1. It contains all 66 chapters of Isaiah on about 24 feet of stitched leather, and it is dated to around 125 BC. Before 1947, the oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament were medieval copies, such as the Aleppo Codex (about AD 930) and the Leningrad Codex (AD 1008). The Isaiah scroll pushed the evidence back by roughly a thousand years.

When scholars compared it with the medieval text, they found the two agree closely. Most differences are spelling, small grammar changes, or copying slips. A few readings are more significant, and modern Bible translations sometimes use the Dead Sea Scrolls to clarify a difficult verse. For example, several translations of Isaiah 53:11 follow the Qumran reading that the servant will "see light" after his suffering.

Why it matters

For readers of the Bible, the scrolls show how carefully Jewish scribes copied Scripture over many centuries. They also give a window into Jewish life and hope in the generations just before and during the time of Jesus, including expectations of a coming Messiah.

The scrolls were likely hidden in the caves before the Romans destroyed the Qumran settlement around AD 68, during the Jewish revolt against Rome. Whoever hid them treasured these writings enough to protect them in the desert. Isaiah 40:8 says the word of our God stands forever, and a copy of that very verse sat in a cave for about two thousand years.
