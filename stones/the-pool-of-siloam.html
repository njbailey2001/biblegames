The Pool of Siloam

This reading opens on Tuesday, November 10, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

In 2004, a sewer repair in Jerusalem uncovered the steps of the pool where a blind man washed and came back seeing.

Read alongside: John 9:1–41; Isaiah 8:6; Nehemiah 3:15

Site file
What
A large stepped pool from the late Second Temple period
Dated
Built around the 1st century BC; used until about AD 70
Found
City of David, Jerusalem, June 2004, during sewer repairs
Excavators
Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron, Israel Antiquities Authority

John 9 tells of a man blind from birth whom Jesus met in Jerusalem. Jesus spat on the ground, made mud, put it on the man's eyes, and told him, "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam." John adds that Siloam means "Sent." The man went, washed, and came back able to see (John 9:6–7).

Looking for the right pool

For a long time, visitors were shown a small pool at the end of Hezekiah's Tunnel as the Pool of Siloam. That pool was built in the Byzantine period, around the fifth century AD, centuries after Jesus. Scholars knew the pool of Jesus' day had to be nearby, but no one had found it.

A lucky discovery

In June 2004, workers were repairing a sewer pipe in the southern part of the City of David, the oldest section of Jerusalem. When they uncovered several ancient stone steps, archaeologists Eli Shukron and Ronny Reich were called in. Their excavation revealed the corner of a large pool lined with steps. The steps were built in sets of five with landings between them, so people could walk down into the water at any level.

The excavators dated the pool by what they found in it. Coins of Alexander Jannaeus, a Jewish king who ruled in the early first century BC, were found in the plaster of an early stage of the pool. Coins from the Jewish revolt against Rome (AD 66–70) were found in the latest layers. So the pool was in use during the lifetime of Jesus, and it was buried after the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70.

What the pool was for

The pool was fed by water that flowed through Hezekiah's Tunnel from the Gihon Spring. Because it held fresh, flowing water, many scholars think it served as a large mikveh, a pool for ritual purification. Pilgrims coming to Jerusalem for festivals would wash here before going up to the temple. A stepped street, which archaeologists have also uncovered, led up from the pool toward the Temple Mount.

This setting adds depth to John 9. Jesus sent a man who had been considered unclean and excluded, not just to any water, but to the pool where worshipers purified themselves before approaching God. And John points out that Siloam means "Sent," the same word John uses many times to describe Jesus as the one sent by the Father.

Still being uncovered

Only part of the pool has been excavated so far, because much of the area is covered by later buildings and a garden. In recent years, the Israel Antiquities Authority has begun work to expose more of it. One day visitors may be able to see the whole pool where the blind man washed.
