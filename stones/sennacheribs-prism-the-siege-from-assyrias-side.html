Sennacherib's Prism: The Siege from Assyria's Side

This reading opens on Monday, November 2, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

The Assyrian king boasted about trapping Hezekiah in Jerusalem. What he left out is just as interesting.

Read alongside: 2 Kings 18–19; Isaiah 36–37; 2 Chronicles 32

Object file
What
Six-sided clay prisms recording Sennacherib's military campaigns, plus stone wall reliefs of Lachish
Dated
Campaign of 701 BC; prisms written c. 691–689 BC
Found
Nineveh, in modern Iraq (Taylor Prism bought in 1830; Lachish reliefs excavated by Austen Henry Layard in the 1840s)
Now at
British Museum, London (Taylor Prism and Lachish reliefs); other copies in Chicago and Jerusalem

In 701 BC, King Sennacherib of Assyria marched west to crush a rebellion. Among the rebels was Hezekiah, king of Judah. The Bible tells this story in three places: 2 Kings 18–19, Isaiah 36–37 and 2 Chronicles 32. Remarkably, Sennacherib told his own version too, and it has survived.

The prisms

Assyrian kings recorded their deeds on clay prisms, six- or eight-sided columns covered in cuneiform writing. Several copies of Sennacherib's annals exist. The best known is the Taylor Prism, acquired at Nineveh by a British officer, Colonel Robert Taylor, in 1830 and now in the British Museum. Others are at the Oriental Institute in Chicago and the Israel Museum in Jerusalem.

In the section on his third campaign, Sennacherib says that Hezekiah of Judah did not submit to him. He claims he besieged and captured 46 of Hezekiah's strong walled cities and countless small villages, and took many people captive. Then comes his most famous line: he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem, his royal city, "like a bird in a cage."

Tribute, but no conquest

Sennacherib goes on to list a large tribute Hezekiah sent to Nineveh, including 30 talents of gold along with silver and other treasures. 2 Kings 18:14 also records that Hezekiah paid tribute, and gives the same amount of gold: 30 talents.

What Sennacherib does not say is that he captured Jerusalem. Assyrian annals loved to describe conquered capitals and executed rebel kings. Here there is only a siege and a boast. Hezekiah stayed on his throne.

The Bible explains why. 2 Kings 19 records Hezekiah praying in the temple and Isaiah prophesying that the Assyrian king would not enter the city or shoot an arrow there. Then the angel of the Lord struck the Assyrian camp, and Sennacherib went home to Nineveh (2 Kings 19:35–36). Not surprisingly, his own annals do not mention a disaster.

The Lachish reliefs

Sennacherib was clearly prouder of another victory: Lachish, Judah's second most important city. 2 Kings 18:14 says Sennacherib was at Lachish when Hezekiah sent his message. In his palace at Nineveh, Sennacherib decorated a room with carved stone panels showing the siege of Lachish in detail: siege ramps, battering rams, archers, and families of Judah being led away. Archaeologists at Lachish in Israel have found the actual Assyrian siege ramp and hundreds of arrowheads.

Taken together, the Bible, the prism and the reliefs give a rare three-way view of one event. They agree on the main facts: Judah was devastated, Lachish fell, Hezekiah paid tribute, and Jerusalem survived.
