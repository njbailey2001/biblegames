The Book of Ruth

This reading opens on Friday, October 30, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A four-chapter story of loss, loyalty and redemption that leads straight to King David.

Read alongside: Ruth 1–4; Matthew 1:5

Book at a glance
Setting
Bethlehem and Moab, "in the days when the judges ruled" (Ruth 1:1)
Length
4 chapters, 85 verses
Main people
Naomi, Ruth, Boaz
Key idea
God's faithful care through ordinary people's loyalty

The Book of Ruth opens in a dark time. The days of the judges were marked by chaos, and on top of that a famine struck Bethlehem. A man named Elimelech took his wife Naomi and their two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, east across the Jordan to the country of Moab. There the sons married Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. Then, within about ten years, Elimelech and both sons died. Naomi was left with no husband, no sons, and no way to support herself.

Chapter 1: "Where you go, I will go"

Naomi heard that the famine in Judah had ended and decided to return home. She urged her daughters-in-law to stay in Moab and remarry. Orpah tearfully agreed, but Ruth refused. Her words in Ruth 1:16–17 are some of the most famous in the Bible: wherever Naomi went, Ruth would go; Naomi's people would be her people, and Naomi's God her God. Back in Bethlehem, Naomi told the townspeople to call her Mara, meaning "bitter," because she felt the Lord had dealt bitterly with her.

Chapter 2: Gleaning in Boaz's field

The two widows arrived at the start of the barley harvest. The Law of Moses told farmers not to harvest the edges of their fields or pick up what fell, so the poor and foreigners could gather it (Leviticus 19:9–10). Ruth went out to glean and happened to work in the field of Boaz, a wealthy relative of Elimelech. Boaz had heard of Ruth's loyalty to Naomi. He protected her, fed her, and quietly told his workers to leave extra grain for her.

Chapter 3: At the threshing floor

Naomi saw that Boaz was a "kinsman-redeemer" (Hebrew go'el), a close relative with the right and duty to help a family in trouble, including buying back family land. Following Naomi's instructions, Ruth went to the threshing floor at night, lay at Boaz's feet, and asked him to spread his garment over her, a request for marriage and protection. Boaz praised her character and promised to act, but there was a relative more closely related who had the first right.

Chapter 4: The redeemer acts

At the town gate, where legal business was done before the elders, Boaz offered the closer relative the chance to redeem Elimelech's land. The man wanted the land until he learned that marrying Ruth came with it, which could affect his own inheritance. He declined and gave Boaz his sandal to seal the deal. Boaz married Ruth, and they had a son named Obed. The women of Bethlehem rejoiced with Naomi, whose emptiness had turned to fullness.

The book ends with a short family line: Obed was the father of Jesse, and Jesse was the father of David. A Moabite widow became the great-grandmother of Israel's greatest king. Matthew 1:5 names Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus, making her story part of the larger story of redemption.
