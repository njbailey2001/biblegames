The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III

This reading opens on Wednesday, November 18, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

An Assyrian monument shows tribute brought from Jehu, king of Israel, the earliest known picture connected to an Israelite king.

Read alongside: 2 Kings 9–10

Object file
What
A black limestone pillar about 6.5 feet tall with carved panels and writing
Dated
About 825 BC
Found
Nimrud (ancient Kalhu), Iraq, by Austen Henry Layard in 1846
Now at
The British Museum, London

In 1846, the explorer Austen Henry Layard was digging at Nimrud, the site of the ancient Assyrian city of Kalhu in northern Iraq. His workers uncovered a tall black stone pillar lying on its side. It was carved on all four sides, with pictures and long lines of cuneiform writing.

A king's record

The pillar is now called the Black Obelisk. It was made for Shalmaneser III, who ruled Assyria from 858 to 824 BC. The inscription lists his military campaigns year by year. Around the top are five rows of carved panels. Each row shows a different ruler or region bringing tribute to the Assyrian king: precious metals, animals such as camels and monkeys, and other goods.

Jehu, king of Israel

The second row of panels is the most famous for readers of the Bible. It shows a man kneeling with his face to the ground before Shalmaneser. Behind him, servants carry gifts. The caption above reads, "The tribute of Jehu, son of Omri." It lists silver, gold, a golden bowl, golden vessels, golden buckets, tin, and a staff for the king's hand.

Jehu was a commander in Israel's army who became king about 841 BC. 2 Kings 9 and 10 tell how he was anointed by a messenger of the prophet Elisha and took the throne. Jehu was not actually a descendant of Omri. The Assyrians commonly referred to the kingdom of Israel as the "house of Omri," after the powerful king who had founded Samaria as Israel's capital.

A unique picture

The Black Obelisk is the earliest known picture connected to a king of Israel. No other carving from ancient times shows an Israelite king or his representative by name. It also helps fix the timeline of the period, because Assyrian records can be dated precisely, and Shalmaneser's records place Jehu's tribute in 841 BC.

What the Bible says

The Bible does not mention Jehu paying tribute to Assyria, but it does say that Israel faced growing pressure from foreign powers in his days. 2 Kings 10:32 says that "in those days the Lord began to cut Israel short," as Hazael of Aram took territory east of the Jordan.

The Black Obelisk stands in the British Museum today, near the Lachish reliefs. Together they show how the kings of Israel and Judah lived in the shadow of the great Assyrian Empire.
