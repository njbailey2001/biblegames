The Tel Dan Stele and the House of David

This reading opens on Saturday, October 31, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

A broken victory monument from northern Israel contains the earliest known mention of King David's dynasty outside the Bible.

Read alongside: 2 Samuel 7:16; 2 Kings 8:7–15; 2 Kings 9

Object file
What
Fragments of a basalt victory inscription in Old Aramaic
Dated
9th century BC (c. 840s BC)
Found
Tel Dan, northern Israel, 1993–1994, by Avraham Biran's excavation team
Now at
Israel Museum, Jerusalem

Before 1993, no ancient inscription outside the Bible was known to mention King David. That changed in the summer of 1993 at Tel Dan, an ancient city near the springs of the Jordan River in far northern Israel.

A discovery in a wall

Archaeologist Avraham Biran had been excavating Dan for decades. In July 1993, surveyor Gila Cook noticed a piece of basalt with writing on it, reused as a building stone in a later wall near the city gate. Two smaller fragments of the same monument turned up in 1994. Together they form part of a stele, an upright stone monument a king set up to celebrate his victories.

The inscription is written in Old Aramaic, the language of the kingdom of Aram based in Damascus. The king's name is missing from the surviving pieces, but the inscription is generally attributed to Hazael of Damascus, who ruled in the second half of the ninth century BC. The Bible mentions Hazael several times as an enemy of Israel (2 Kings 8:7–15; 10:32; 13:3).

The key words

The surviving text says the speaker killed a king of Israel and a king of the "house of David." The phrase is written as bytdwd, meaning "House of David," the way ancient people referred to a royal dynasty. The partly preserved royal names are usually restored as Jehoram (or Joram) son of Ahab, king of Israel, and Ahaziah, king of Judah.

This means that about 150 years after David's time, a foreign enemy king referred to the kingdom of Judah by the name of its founding dynasty. People do not name a royal house after someone who never existed. The stele does not prove every detail of David's life, but it is strong evidence that David was a real, historical king who founded a lasting dynasty, just as God promised in 2 Samuel 7:16.

Comparing with 2 Kings

The stele was later smashed, probably when Israel retook Dan, and its pieces were reused as building material. That is how it survived for nearly three thousand years, waiting in a wall until 1993.
