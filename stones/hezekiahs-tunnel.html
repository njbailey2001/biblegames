Hezekiah's Tunnel

This reading opens on Sunday, November 1, 2026. Come back that day to read it and take the quiz.

Go to today's reading

Two crews dug through solid rock from opposite ends to save Jerusalem's water supply. An ancient inscription tells how they met.

Read alongside: 2 Kings 20:20; 2 Chronicles 32:2–4, 30

Site file
What
A water tunnel about 533 m (1,750 ft) long, plus the Siloam Inscription
Dated
Late 8th century BC, reign of Hezekiah (c. 715–686 BC)
Where
City of David, Jerusalem: from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam
Inscription now at
Istanbul Archaeology Museums, Turkey

Ancient cities lived or died by their water. Jerusalem's main source was the Gihon Spring, which sat outside and below the city walls in the Kidron Valley. In a siege, an enemy could cut the city off from its spring. King Hezekiah of Judah faced exactly that danger.

The Assyrian threat

In the late 700s BC, the Assyrian Empire was the superpower of the Near East. It had already destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC. Hezekiah rebelled against Assyria, and he knew an attack was coming. 2 Chronicles 32:2–4 says he stopped up the springs outside the city so the Assyrian kings would not find water, and verse 30 says he directed the waters of the upper Gihon down to the west side of the City of David. 2 Kings 20:20 also mentions how he made the pool and the conduit and brought water into the city.

Digging from both ends

The tunnel still exists, and visitors can walk through it today in knee-deep water. It winds in an S-shaped path for about 533 meters (roughly 1,750 feet) through solid limestone, from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam inside the city. The drop from one end to the other is only about 30 centimeters, a remarkably gentle slope.

Two teams of workers started at opposite ends and dug toward each other with picks. The inscription says that as they got close, they could hear each other's voices through the rock.

The Siloam Inscription

In 1880, a boy wading in the tunnel noticed letters carved into the wall near the Siloam end. The inscription, written in early Hebrew script, describes the moment of breakthrough. In paraphrase: while the diggers were still swinging their picks toward each other, with three cubits left, a man's voice was heard calling to his fellow. Then the crews struck pick against pick, the water flowed from the spring to the pool, about 1,200 cubits, and the rock above their heads was about 100 cubits high.

The inscription does not name Hezekiah, which has led to some discussion. But its letter shapes fit the late eighth century BC, and the tunnel matches the biblical description well. In 1891 the inscription was cut out of the wall and taken to Istanbul, then the capital of the Ottoman Empire that ruled Jerusalem. It remains there today.

Why it matters

The tunnel is one of the clearest places where the Bible and archaeology meet. It shows a king preparing for war in practical ways while also, as 2 Chronicles 32 records, calling his people to trust the Lord. When the Assyrians arrived in 701 BC, Jerusalem held out. Tomorrow's reading looks at that siege from the Assyrian side.
