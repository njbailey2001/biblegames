Acts 17: Paul in Thessalonica, Berea and Athens

Three cities, three responses, and a sermon on a hill where Paul quoted Greek poets.

Read alongside: Acts 17:1–34

Chapter at a glance
Setting
Macedonia and Greece, about AD 50, Paul's second missionary journey
Main people
Paul, Silas, Timothy, Jason, Dionysius, Damaris
Places
Thessalonica, Berea, Athens (Areopagus)
Related evidence
Inscriptions using the title "politarch"; ancient reports of Athenian altars to unknown gods

Acts 17 follows Paul and his companions as they move south through Greece. In each city the gospel meets a different response.

Thessalonica: turning the world upside down

In Thessalonica, a major city on the Roman road called the Via Egnatia, Paul went to the synagogue for three Sabbaths. He reasoned from the Scriptures that the Messiah had to suffer and rise from the dead, and that Jesus was the Messiah. Some Jews and many God-fearing Greeks and leading women believed.

Others became jealous and started a riot. Not finding Paul, they dragged a believer named Jason before the city officials, shouting that these men had "turned the world upside down" and were saying there is another king, Jesus (Acts 17:6–7).

Luke calls the city officials politarchs. The title is rare in Greek literature, but archaeologists have found dozens of inscriptions using it, many from Thessalonica itself. One, from a Roman arch called the Vardar Gate, is now in the British Museum. Luke used the exact local term.

Berea: eager and careful

Paul and Silas were sent away by night to Berea. Luke says the Jews there were more noble-minded than those in Thessalonica, because they received the message eagerly and examined the Scriptures every day to see if these things were true (Acts 17:11). Many believed. When opponents arrived from Thessalonica, Paul was sent on to Athens.

Athens: the unknown god

Athens was still a famous center of philosophy, though past its political peak. Paul was troubled to see the city full of idols. He spoke in the synagogue and in the marketplace, where he debated Epicurean and Stoic philosophers. Some called him a "babbler." They brought him to the Areopagus, a council that took its name from the Hill of Ares, to explain his new teaching.

Paul began respectfully. He said he noticed an altar inscribed "To an unknown god," and that he would proclaim the God they worshiped without knowing. Ancient writers such as Pausanias and Philostratus also mention altars to unknown gods at Athens.

Paul then taught that the Creator of the world does not live in temples made by human hands and needs nothing from us. He made every nation from one man and set their times and places, so that people would seek him. To make his point, Paul quoted Greek writers: "In him we live and move and have our being," and "We are his offspring." The second line comes from the poet Aratus, who lived in the third century BC.

Paul closed by saying God now commands all people everywhere to repent, because he has fixed a day to judge the world by a man he appointed, and has proved it by raising him from the dead. At the mention of resurrection, some mocked. Others said they wanted to hear more. A few believed, including Dionysius, a member of the Areopagus, and a woman named Damaris.
